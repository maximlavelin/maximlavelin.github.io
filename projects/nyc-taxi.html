NYC Taxi Fleet Optimizer: Predicting Hourly Urban Demand with Weather Integration

Result: XGBoost reduced Mean Absolute Error by ~25% over a linear baseline (728 → 546 rides/hour) on a strict chronological hold-out — with the largest gains during peak-hour demand, exactly when accurate forecasting matters most operationally.

The problem

NYC taxi demand swings from near-zero to over 12,000 rides/hour within a single day. Under-forecasting means long passenger wait times and lost revenue; over-forecasting means wasted idle mileage. I framed this as a supervised regression problem — predict total citywide pickups for a given hour — using a year of NYC TLC trip data merged with JFK weather observations.

Data

  • 8,616 hourly observations across 2024, aggregated from NYC TLC Yellow Taxi trip records
  • Demand is highly volatile: mean 4,708 rides/hour, std dev 2,712, ranging from 0 to 12,757
  • Weather data from JFK Airport (NOAA/NCEI) — reduced from ~125 raw meteorological fields down to two that actually mattered operationally: continuous temperature and a binary rain indicator (precipitation intensity didn't matter — presence of rain did)

Approach

Two models, evaluated the same way, to make the case for non-linearity honestly rather than assuming it:

  • Linear Regression baseline — interpretable, additive combination of temporal, weather, and lagged demand features. Captures broad seasonality but structurally can't model regime-dependent spikes.
  • XGBoost — gradient-boosted trees to capture non-linear interactions (e.g., demand responding differently to weather depending on hour-of-day or recent history)

Feature set: hour-of-day, day-of-week, weekend flag, temperature, rain flag, and lagged demand (24h and 168h/weekly lags, rolling means) — the lagged features turned out to dominate.

Evaluation — why the split matters

I used a strict chronological split (train: Jan–Sep 2024, validation: Oct 2024, test: Nov–Dec 2024) instead of random k-fold. This mirrors how the model would actually be deployed — predicting the future from only the past — and avoids the data leakage that a random split would silently introduce.

Results

ModelMAE (rides/hr)RMSE
Linear Regression728.43993.47
XGBoost546.19810.55

The linear model systematically underestimates peak demand — its additive structure can't capture the sharp, regime-dependent transitions of rush hour. XGBoost closely tracks both the magnitude and timing of these swings. Feature importance analysis shows the 168-hour (weekly) lag dominates predictive power, followed by the 24h lag and hour-of-day — weather contributes only secondary, contextual signal.

What I'd do differently

Weather from a single airport station is a coarse proxy for citywide conditions, and city-level aggregation hides neighborhood variation — a zone-level model (Manhattan vs. outer boroughs separately) would likely outperform this citywide one. I'd also incorporate event data (concerts, sports games) to catch non-seasonal demand shocks, currently the hardest cases for the model to predict.
