Gaps Between Public Transport Need and Accessibility in Urban Israel

GIS & AI · Dr. Asnat Mangel2025–2026CBS Census 2022 + GTFS
Combined CBS Census 2022 with national GTFS feeds across 2,179 urban statistical areas in 142 localities, building separate need and supply indices and measuring the gap. The headline finding inverts the intuitive assumption: physical stop coverage is near-universal, so service frequency is what actually separates well-served areas from underserved ones.

Problem

Transit planning needs a defensible definition of “underserved” rather than a visual impression of a sparse map. Following Currie’s (2010) methodology, this project calculates need and supply separately and then measures the gap between them, so that the areas identified are those where high dependence on public transport coincides with low actual service.

Data and method

Three sources: the CBS 2022 statistical-area layer, the 2022 population census, and nationwide GTFS feeds from Israel’s Ministry of Transport.

  • Need index built from share of households without a car, share of residents aged 65+, population density, and median income — the variables that capture dependence on public transport rather than mere population size.
  • Supply index from 400-meter buffers around stops combined with departures per capita on a representative weekday, so frequency counts rather than just proximity.
  • Getis-Ord Gi* hot-spot analysis to separate statistically significant clusters from incidental variation, run in Python and then independently re-run in ArcGIS Pro as a validation step.
  • Three GeoJSON layers exported to ArcGIS Pro for cartography, then published to ArcGIS Online as an interactive map.

Findings

The analysis covered 2,179 urban statistical areas across 142 localities. The most useful result was not the one I expected: median stop coverage is approximately 100% and mean coverage about 94%, meaning physical access to a stop is essentially solved in urban Israel. What separates areas is service frequency — how many departures actually serve that stop.

168 statistical areas were classified as high need / low accessibility. The strongest spatial concentration is in Tel Aviv–Yafo, where 121 of 161 areas (about 75%) are hot spots, with further clusters in Jerusalem and in northern and southern urban areas. Cold spots — a better match between need and supply — appear in Netanya, Bnei Brak, and several Arab localities including Umm al-Fahm, Tayibe, Tamra, Sakhnin and Tira.

Because dense central areas show substantial gaps alongside peripheral ones, the pattern cannot be reduced to a center-versus-periphery divide — which is the finding most directly relevant to how service improvements would be prioritized.

Limitations

The analysis uses scheduled GTFS data rather than observed performance, so a route that is timetabled but chronically late counts as served. It also assumes a uniform 400-meter walking distance regardless of terrain or road network, and the demographic inputs are 2022 figures that do not reflect later changes to either population or the transport network.
